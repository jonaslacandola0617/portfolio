## Going in with the wrong expectation

I expected Course 1 to be mostly definitions — CIA triad, a glossary of attack types, maybe some
compliance framework names to memorize. Some of that is in there, but the more useful thread was
learning how a SOC actually *thinks* about risk, not just naming threats.

## What stuck

<Callout type="tip" title="The reframe that helped most">
  Security isn't about eliminating risk — it's about reducing it to a level the organization has
  decided it can accept. Every control is a trade-off against cost, usability, or speed.
</Callout>

A few specific things I'll actually carry forward:

- **The CIA triad as a diagnostic, not a definition.** When evaluating a control, asking "which of
  confidentiality, integrity, or availability does this protect, and what does it trade off?" is
  more useful than reciting the acronym.
- **Frameworks exist to standardize communication**, not to add paperwork for its own sake. NIST
  CSF gives a shared vocabulary so a security team and an auditor (or a new hire) are talking about
  the same thing.
- **A SOC analyst's day is mostly triage.** Most alerts are false positives; the skill is knowing
  which ones deserve a closer look, fast.

## Where it connected to the networking side

Course 1 spends real time on network fundamentals — ports, protocols, firewalls — which lined up
almost exactly with what CCNA was covering the same month. Working through both at once made each
one click faster than either would have alone; a routing lab makes "an attacker scanning open
ports" concrete instead of abstract.

## What I'd tell someone starting Course 1

Don't rush the CIA triad and risk management sections to get to "the interesting parts" like
incident response — they're the lens everything after this gets viewed through, and skimming them
means re-reading later.
